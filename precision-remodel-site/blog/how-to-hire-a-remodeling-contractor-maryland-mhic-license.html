How to Hire a Remodeling Contractor in Maryland and Check an MHIC License

November 18, 2026  ·  10 min read

Search the contractor's name or MHIC number on the Maryland Department of Labor's license lookup at labor.maryland.gov. Confirm the license is active, the name matches your contract, and look for any complaint or disciplinary history. You can practice on ours: Precision Remodel is MHIC #151439.

How do you check an MHIC license in Maryland?

You check it on the state's own database, not on the contractor's website or a review page. The Maryland Home Improvement Commission (MHIC) sits under the Maryland Department of Labor, and the license search is on labor.maryland.gov. Searching for "MHIC license lookup" from that site will get you to it.

Here is the process I'd follow for any contractor, including us:

  1. Get the number in writing. A licensed contractor should be able to give you an MHIC number without hesitation. It should also appear on the contract and estimate.
  2. Search by number, then by name. Search by license number first. Then search by the person's or company's name to make sure the two agree.
  3. Check the status. You want to see an active license, not expired, suspended or revoked.
  4. Match the names. The name on the license should match the name on your contract and the business you are paying. If a company says it is "licensed" but the license belongs to a different person or business, ask why.
  5. Look for history. The record can show whether the commission has taken action against the licensee. If something is unclear, call the commission and ask rather than guessing.

What does the MHIC license do and not tell you?

It tells you the contractor is licensed by the state to do home improvement work. It does not tell you the contractor is good, insured, available, or the right fit for your project.

The license is a floor. It is a reason to keep talking, not a reason to sign.

The MHIC license tells you

  • The state licenses this person or business
  • Whether the license is active
  • Whether the commission has taken action
  • Who to contact if there is a dispute
  • A name to match against your contract

It does not tell you

  • Whether the work quality is high
  • Whether they carry insurance
  • Whether they handle your type of project well
  • Whether the price or schedule is realistic
  • Whether electrical or plumbing is licensed separately

One more point: in Maryland, electrical and plumbing work generally have their own licensing. Ask who is doing that part of your kitchen or bathroom and ask for their license. If you are unsure what applies, the Department of Labor can tell you.

If a homeowner is harmed by a licensed contractor, the state has a process for complaints and a guaranty fund for certain losses. The eligibility rules and limits are set by the state and can change, so ask the commission directly rather than relying on a summary from a contractor, including this one.

What questions should you ask before you sign?

Ask questions that reveal how the contractor actually runs a job, not whether they sound confident. Here are the ones I'd ask, grouped by what they tell you.

Scope and price

  • What is included and what is not? Who buys the cabinets, tile, fixtures and appliances?
  • How are unknowns handled? Behind a kitchen or bathroom wall there are often surprises, such as old wiring, rot or out-of-level framing. Ask how a change order works and how it is priced before it happens.
  • Is the estimate itemized enough for you to compare it with another bid? Pricing always depends on scope, so a number with no scope behind it is not useful.

Who does the work

  • Who will be on site day to day? Is the work done by employees or subcontractors?
  • Who is the licensed electrician and plumber?
  • Who is your point of contact when something comes up?

Schedule

  • What is the realistic start date, and what could move it? Lead times for cabinets and special-order items often drive the schedule. We cover that in how long a kitchen remodel takes in Baltimore County.

Walls and structure

  • If the plan involves opening up a wall, ask how they decide whether it is load-bearing. A good contractor will tell you when an engineer is needed and will not guess. See how to tell if a wall is load-bearing and removing a load-bearing wall: cost, permits and process.

Inspection

  • What do you expect the inspector to look at on this job, and how do you prepare for it? We have an inspector's background, so we build with that in mind. The answer to this question tells you whether a contractor builds to pass inspection or hopes for the best.

What about permits, insurance and contract basics in Maryland?

Get all three sorted before work starts: the permits, proof of insurance, and a written contract. Details vary by county and by project, so confirm them with the people who set the rules.

Permits. Many kitchen and bathroom projects need permits, especially when they involve electrical, plumbing, structural changes or layout changes. Whether your project does depends on the scope and on your jurisdiction. In Baltimore County, ask the county's permits office. In other counties and in Baltimore City, ask the local permitting agency.

Ask your contractor who pulls the permit and who is responsible for scheduling inspections. Be cautious about any contractor who suggests skipping the permit to save time or money. Unpermitted work can create problems later, including when you sell.

Insurance. An MHIC license is not proof of insurance. Ask for a certificate of insurance, and ask your insurance agent what you need to see for a project of your size. If anyone working on your property is hurt or something is damaged, you want to know who is covered. Ask about workers' compensation as well as liability coverage.

The contract. Maryland requires home improvement contracts to be in writing and to include certain things, such as the contractor's MHIC number. Rules also cover how much a contractor can ask for as a down payment. Because the details are set by state law and can change, confirm the current requirements with the Maryland Home Improvement Commission before you sign. At minimum, a good contract should spell out:

  • The full scope of work and the materials, by brand and model where it matters
  • The total price and the payment schedule, tied to progress rather than dates
  • Start and estimated completion timing
  • How changes are requested, priced and approved
  • Who handles permits and inspections
  • The contractor's name and MHIC number matching the lookup

If your project involves structure, such as removing a wall, the contract should say who is bringing in the engineer. We never present structural guidance as an engineering opinion; that is the engineer's call.

How do you read references and past projects?

Treat references as a way to learn how the contractor behaves when things get difficult, not to hear that the finished room looks nice. Online ratings are a starting point, but they are the easiest thing to skew and the hardest to verify.

A few practical ways to get more out of them:

  • Ask for recent projects similar to yours. A contractor who does mostly decks may not be the best fit for a primary bath.
  • Call and ask specific questions. Did the crew show up when scheduled? How were surprises handled? Was the site kept clean? Did the final price match the contract plus approved changes?
  • Ask to see work in person if possible. Photos are useful. A completed room is better. Look at the details: tile lines, grout, trim joints, cabinet alignment.
  • Check the license record again. A clean record doesn't prove quality, but a complaint history is useful information.
  • Be careful with before-and-after photos. Ask whether the contractor did the work shown and whether it was their crew.

What red flags point to the wrong contractor?

The clearest red flags are a contractor who cannot or will not show a license number, asks for a large payment up front, or avoids putting things in writing.

  • No MHIC number, or a number that does not match the name. Walk away until it is resolved.
  • Pressure to sign today. A real estimate should still be good tomorrow.
  • A large down payment or cash-only terms. Maryland limits down payments, so confirm the current rule with the commission.
  • "We don't need a permit." Verify that with the county yourself.
  • A vague scope. "Kitchen remodel, one price" with no detail leaves room for disputes.
  • A bid far below the others. It often means something was left out, and you will see it later as a change order.
  • No written contract, or one that is missing the license number.
  • Reluctance to give references or show a certificate of insurance.
  • Dismissing structural questions. If a wall might be load-bearing and the answer is "it'll be fine," ask for an engineer. Read what makes a wall load-bearing before you talk to anyone.

No single item proves a contractor is bad. A pattern of them should make you slow down.

FAQ

How do I check a Maryland contractor's MHIC license?

Use the license lookup on the Maryland Department of Labor's website (labor.maryland.gov). Search by license number and by name, confirm the status is active, and make sure the name matches your contract. If anything doesn't match, ask the contractor and the commission before you pay anything.

Is an MHIC license the same as insurance?

No. The license shows the state has licensed the contractor. It does not prove the contractor carries liability or workers' compensation insurance. Ask for a certificate of insurance and confirm what coverage you should expect for your project.

Do I need a permit for a kitchen or bathroom remodel in Baltimore County?

It depends on the scope. Projects that involve electrical, plumbing or structural changes often need permits, but requirements vary by project and jurisdiction. Ask Baltimore County's permits office, and ask your contractor who will pull the permit and schedule inspections.

Does a Maryland contractor license mean the work will be good?

Not by itself. It is a baseline that tells you the contractor is licensed. Quality shows up in references, completed work you can see in person, a detailed written scope, and how the contractor handles questions about permits and inspections.
