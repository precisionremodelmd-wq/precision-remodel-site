## What decides what a kitchen or bathroom remodel costs?

Four things decide it: how much you change, what you choose to install, what the house hides, and what the county requires. Everything else is a variation on those.

Here is how the major drivers compare:

- **Scope:** Cosmetic refresh vs. full gut. Who to ask: your contractor.
- **Materials:** Stock, semi-custom or custom; tile and slab choices. Who to ask: cabinet and tile suppliers.
- **Layout changes:** Moving sinks, walls, appliances, windows. Who to ask: contractor, plus an engineer for structure.
- **House condition:** Old wiring, plumbing, subfloor, hidden damage. Who to ask: contractor after opening or inspecting.
- **Permits and inspections:** Trade permits, review, required inspections. Who to ask: Baltimore County permits office.
- **Allowances and changes:** Selections made late or changed mid-job. Who to ask: your written contract.

Notice that none of these is about square footage alone. A small bathroom with high-end tile, a custom vanity and a relocated shower can cost more per square foot than a large, simple kitchen refresh.

## Why does pricing depend on scope, and how do I read any range I see?

A range you see online is an average of very different projects, so it tells you little about yours. Read it by asking what is included and what is not.

When you see a published range for a kitchen or bathroom, check these before trusting it:

- **Is it a refresh or a gut?** Painting cabinets and swapping counters is a different job from removing everything to the studs.
- **Does it include design, permits and demolition disposal?** Some figures leave these out.
- **What grade of materials does it assume?** Most national averages assume mid-range finishes. A high-end kitchen with custom cabinetry, stone counters and professional-grade appliances sits well outside that.
- **Does it assume the layout stays?** Layout changes are one of the largest swing factors.
- **Is it for your market?** Labor and supply conditions in the Baltimore area differ from the national picture.

A useful habit: write down your own scope in plain language before you ask anyone for a price. For example: keep the layout, replace cabinets and counters, new floor, new lighting, move nothing. Contractors can then price the same job, and you can compare estimates fairly. ## Where does the money go on a high-end kitchen or bathroom?

In a high-end project, the biggest share typically goes to cabinetry, counters, tile and fixtures, plus the skilled labor to install them well. These are also the areas where your choices have the most control over the total.

**Cabinets.** In a kitchen, cabinetry is usually the largest single material line. Stock, semi-custom and fully custom cabinets differ in price, lead time and how well they fit an irregular room. Custom work can solve awkward older-home layouts, but it costs more and takes longer to order.

**Counters.** Material, thickness, edge profile, number of seams, and cutouts (sink, cooktop) all affect the price. Natural stone slabs vary a lot from slab to slab, so ask your fabricator what is included.

**Tile.** Tile cost is not only the tile. Large-format tile, intricate patterns, niches, and full-height walls add labor because the substrate has to be flatter and cuts take longer. In a bathroom, a custom tiled shower with a waterproofing system is a labor-intensive item that homeowners often underestimate.

**Fixtures and appliances.** Faucets, shower systems, lighting, and appliances range from basic to premium. Some premium appliances also need special venting, gas or electrical supply, or custom panels, which add cost beyond the sticker price.

**Labor and finish detail.** Trim, built-ins, and clean tile layouts take time. A well-run project spends labor where you will see and feel it.

## How do layout changes, plumbing and electrical affect cost?

Moving things costs more than replacing them in place. Every relocated sink, range, or shower means new plumbing, electrical, framing, and finish work, and sometimes a permit.

Common layout changes and what they set off:

- **Moving a sink or dishwasher.** New supply and drain lines, and possibly venting. Opening a floor or wall to reach them adds patching and finish work.
- **Relocating a shower, tub or toilet.** Drain lines sit in the floor structure, so changes can involve cutting joists or the subfloor.
- **Adding circuits.** Modern kitchens often need more dedicated circuits than older houses have. If your electrical panel is full or dated, a panel upgrade may come into the budget. Your electrician and the utility (BGE, for most of Baltimore County) determine what a service change involves.
- **Moving or removing a wall.** This is where cost and risk jump. Never assume a wall is safe to remove. Whether it is load-bearing, and what support replaces it, is a question for a structural engineer, not a rule of thumb. Our articles on [how to tell if a wall is load-bearing](https://precisionremodelingmd.com/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-wall-is-load-bearing) and [removing a load-bearing wall: cost, permits and process](https://precisionremodelingmd.com/blog/removing-a-load-bearing-wall-cost-permits-process) explain what to expect.

An open-concept request is often the single biggest scope change in a kitchen. If you want one, get the structural question answered early, before you set a budget.

## What do older Baltimore County homes tend to need?

Older homes in places like Catonsville, Towson, Dundalk and Parkville often carry costs that you can't see until walls and floors are open. A budget for them should include a contingency for the unexpected.

What tends to turn up in houses of that age, and why we plan for it:

- **Outdated wiring or a small panel.** This can affect how many new circuits and appliances the house can handle.
- **Aging supply and drain piping.** Replacing a vanity or tub can reveal pipes you would rather not reconnect to.
- **Uneven or damaged subfloors.** Long-term leaks around tubs, toilets and sinks can soften the subfloor. Tile needs a solid, flat base.
- **Out-of-plumb walls and floors.** Cabinets and tile need straight lines, so crews may spend extra time shimming, furring or leveling.
- **Older materials.** In homes built before 1978, federal lead-safe work practices apply to contractors disturbing painted surfaces. Some older flooring or wall materials may need testing before demolition. Ask your contractor how they handle both.

Because of our inspection background, we look at these things before writing a scope, and we tell you where we think surprises are most likely. We can't promise nothing will turn up, but a good contingency plan keeps one discovery from derailing the whole budget. If the problem is structural, such as a sagging floor or a settling foundation, that is an engineer's call. ## Are permits and inspections part of the remodel budget?

Yes. Permits and inspections are real line items in the budget, in both fees and time, and what is required depends on the scope.

In general, work such as new electrical circuits, plumbing changes, and structural changes is more likely to need permits than cosmetic updates. Exactly what applies, and what it costs, varies by jurisdiction and by project. Baltimore County's permits office (Permits, Approvals and Inspections) can tell you what your project needs. Baltimore City and neighboring counties have their own offices and rules, so confirm which one applies to your address.

Two practical points:

- **Ask who pulls the permit.** A licensed contractor normally handles this and builds it into the contract. If someone suggests skipping permits to save money, treat that as a warning sign. Unpermitted work can cause trouble when you sell or file an insurance claim.
- **Plan for inspection timing.** Inspections happen at set stages, and the schedule depends on the inspector's availability. Good sequencing keeps your crew from waiting.

## How do I set an allowance and avoid change orders?

Set allowances based on real selections, not guesses, and finalize those selections before work starts. Most change orders come from decisions made late, not from bad luck.

An allowance is a placeholder budget for items you haven't chosen yet, such as tile, plumbing fixtures, lighting, or appliances. If the final choice costs more than the allowance, you pay the difference.

To keep allowances honest:

1. **Shop before you sign.** Visit the showroom, get actual prices for tile, fixtures, and counters, and use those numbers in the contract.
2. **Ask what each allowance includes.** Does the tile allowance cover only material, or also trim pieces and waterproofing products? Is installation labor included?
3. **Check lead times.** Custom cabinets, special-order tile and some appliances take time. Late arrivals delay the whole schedule.
4. **Lock the layout.** Moving a sink after the plumber has roughed in is a classic change order.
5. **Hold a contingency.** Keep a reserve for hidden conditions, especially in older homes. Ask your contractor how they recommend sizing it for your house.
6. **Get changes in writing.** Any change should come with a written price and schedule effect before work proceeds.

When you compare estimates, line them up by scope and allowances, not by the bottom line. A lower bid with thin allowances often ends up costing as much once real selections are made.
