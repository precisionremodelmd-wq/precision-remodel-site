Spend the most on the bathroom your household uses every day and the one a buyer would notice first. In many Baltimore County homes that is the primary bath. If the hall bath is your only full bathroom, or it takes the heaviest daily wear, it moves to the top. Waterproofing and plumbing deserve full budget in either one.

## Which bathroom should get the bigger budget?

The one that solves your biggest daily problem. A bathroom remodel is worth the most where people actually stand, shower and get ready every morning.

For most households, that comes down to three questions:

- **Who uses it?** A primary bath serves one or two adults. A hall bath often serves kids, guests and sometimes everyone in the house.
- **What is failing?** Leaks, soft floors, failing grout and poor ventilation are more urgent than dated finishes, whichever room they are in.
- **Are you staying or selling?** If you plan to stay for years, spend on what you will enjoy. If you plan to sell within a few years, spend on what reads as clean, current and well built.

Pricing depends entirely on scope: the size of the room, whether the layout changes, the fixtures and tile you choose, and what is found behind the walls. Compare rooms by scope, not by label. ## When does the primary bath deserve the splurge?

Put more into the primary bath when it is the room you personally use the most and the one that shapes how buyers see the house. It is where upgrades you can feel make sense.

Typical primary-bath upgrades that pay back in daily use:

- A larger walk-in shower with a proper bench, niche and glass
- A double vanity with better storage
- Heated floors, a quality exhaust fan and better lighting
- Higher-grade tile and a more considered layout

The layout is where cost climbs fastest. Moving a toilet, shower or vanity means moving drains and supply lines. Combining a closet or adjacent space into the bath can involve framing changes. ## When does the hall bath deserve more attention?

The hall bath should come first when it is the only full bathroom, when it is the one guests and children use, or when it is the room showing real damage. Durability matters more here than luxury.

In many of the older homes we see in Essex, Parkville and Perry Hall, the hall bath is the only full bath on the main living level. It takes the most abuse: long showers, wet towels, splashing, and a fan that may not have been running for years. Putting money into this room usually means:

- A properly waterproofed shower or tub surround
- A real exhaust fan vented to the exterior
- Durable, low-maintenance tile and grout choices (see our [tile selection guide for Baltimore County bathrooms](/blog/tile-selection-guide-for-baltimore-county-bathrooms-what-lasts-what-doesn-t))
- A good vanity and fixtures that hold up to heavy use

A hall bath is also easier to keep disciplined. The layout is often a standard tub-in-an-alcove arrangement, so keeping the plumbing where it is can leave more of the budget for things that last.

## Where should you never cut corners, in either bathroom?

Never cut what is hidden behind the finish. Waterproofing, plumbing, electrical and ventilation are the same job in a $ budget bath and a high-end one, and they cause the most expensive failures.

Because of our inspector background, we know what inspectors flag, and we build with those items in mind:

1. **Shower and tub waterproofing.** Beautiful tile over poor waterproofing fails slowly and expensively.
2. **Old supply and drain lines.** If a wall is open and the plumbing is original, replacing it then is far cheaper than opening the wall again later.
3. **Electrical.** Outlets and lighting near water need to be protected and installed to current requirements. Ask your contractor to explain what applies to your project.
4. **Ventilation.** Moisture that has nowhere to go ends up in the framing, the paint and the ceiling below.
5. **Permits and inspections.** Whether your project needs a permit depends on scope and location. Confirm with Baltimore County's permits office rather than assuming.

Older homes can hide surprises such as outdated wiring, corroded pipes or moisture damage. Keep a contingency in the plan rather than spending every dollar on finishes. How much to set aside depends on the age of the house and how much of the room is being opened up.

## Should you remodel both bathrooms at once or one at a time?

It depends on your household and your budget. Doing both together can cut down on repeated setup and give you a consistent look, but it also means more disruption and, if they are your only bathrooms, no working one for a stretch.

A practical way to decide:

- If you have a second full bath elsewhere in the house, doing both at once is workable.
- If one bath is your only full bathroom, phase the work, or plan for the schedule and a temporary arrangement.
- If your budget only covers one, fix the room with the most urgent problem first. Then update the second when it makes sense.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a primary bath remodel always more expensive than a hall bath?

Not always. Scope drives cost. A small, simple primary bath can cost less than a large hall bath with a layout change or plumbing repairs. Compare them by what is included, not by room name.

### Which bathroom adds more resale value?

Buyers tend to notice a clean, updated primary bath, but a dated or damaged hall bath can hurt a sale just as much. A local real estate agent can tell you what buyers in your neighborhood expect.

### Can I keep the existing layout to save money?

Often yes. Keeping fixtures where the plumbing already is generally limits the work behind the walls. Your contractor should tell you what changing the layout would involve.

### Do I need a permit for a bathroom remodel?

It depends on the work being done. Cosmetic updates and plumbing, electrical or structural changes are treated differently. Contact Baltimore County's permits office or ask your licensed contractor to confirm before work starts.

### What if you find a problem when the walls are opened?

A good contractor explains what was found, shows you the options and gives you a written change to the scope before continuing. Older homes make this fairly common, which is why a contingency is worth planning for.
