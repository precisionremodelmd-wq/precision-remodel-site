## Do I need a permit to remodel a kitchen or bathroom in Baltimore County?

If the project touches wiring, plumbing, structure or the layout of the room, plan on a permit. If you are only changing finishes, you probably don't need one, but confirm for your specific project.

The county decides what requires a permit, not your contractor and not a website. Baltimore County's Department of Permits, Approvals and Inspections is where to get a current answer. A short call or a visit with a written scope in hand usually settles it. Ask before you sign a contract, not after demolition.

The same logic answers the statewide question, "Do I need a permit to remodel a kitchen in Maryland?" Maryland leaves much of this to the local jurisdiction, so the answer for your house comes from your county (and, in some towns, a municipality or an HOA with its own rules).

## What remodeling work usually triggers a permit, and what usually doesn't?

Work that changes the electrical system, plumbing, structure or the footprint of the room usually triggers a permit. Work that only changes surfaces usually doesn't. The table below is a planning guide, not a legal list.

| Type of work | Permit likely? |
|---|---|
| Painting, caulking, hardware | Usually no. Cosmetic only. |
| New flooring, tile over existing floor | Usually no. Confirm if subfloor or framing changes. |
| Replacing cabinets in the same layout | Usually no. Confirm if wiring or plumbing moves. |
| New circuits, moved outlets, new lighting runs | Usually yes. Electrical work. |
| Moving or adding sinks, toilets, showers, tubs | Usually yes. Plumbing work. |
| Removing or altering walls | Usually yes. Structural; may need an engineer. |
| Adding a window or enlarging an opening | Often yes. Ask the county. |
| Changing a range hood or exhaust fan route | Ask the county. Depends on the scope. |

A few notes from how these projects tend to play out:

- **A "simple" kitchen refresh often isn't simple.** New cabinets in the same spot is one thing. Add under-cabinet lighting, a new dishwasher circuit and a relocated sink, and you have crossed into permit territory.
- **Bathroom remodels hit plumbing and electrical quickly.** Swapping a tub for a walk-in shower, moving a vanity or adding a heated floor usually involves both trades.
- **Walls are the biggest question mark.** Opening a kitchen to a dining room can involve a load-bearing wall. That needs a structural answer from a licensed engineer, not a guess. ## Who pulls the permit: the homeowner or a licensed contractor?

In most professional remodels, the contractor pulls the permit and manages the inspections. Some jurisdictions allow homeowners to pull permits for their own home, but the rules, and what trades must be licensed, vary, so ask the county.

Here is how to think about it:

- **Contractor-pulled permit:** The contractor is on record for the work, schedules inspections and deals with the county's questions. In Maryland, a home improvement contractor must hold an MHIC license. Precision Remodel is licensed under MHIC #151439.
- **Homeowner-pulled permit:** Possible in some cases, but you become responsible for the work passing inspection. Licensed electricians and plumbers may still be required for parts of the job. Ask the county what applies to your project.
- **Unpermitted work:** This is the one to avoid. It can complicate insurance claims and come up when you sell, when a buyer's inspector or the county asks for records.

If a contractor suggests skipping the permit to save time or money, treat that as a warning sign about the rest of the job.

## How do permits change your scope, timeline and estimate?

Permits add steps before and during construction, so they affect both your schedule and your estimate. Exactly how much depends on scope, the county's current review times and how complete your plans are.

![Open kitchen wall studs with exposed wiring and plumbing lines before drywall, awaiting inspection](/images/blog/open-kitchen-wall-studs-with-exposed-wiring-and-pl-909feff0.jpg "Inspections typically happen while wiring and plumbing are still visible, so the schedule has to leave room before the walls close. (AI-generated illustration)")

What that looks like in practice:

- **Before work:** Your scope gets defined in writing, drawings or a clear plan are prepared, and the application is submitted. Work doesn't start on the regulated portion until the permit is issued.
- **During work:** Inspections happen at set points, commonly before walls are closed up over wiring and plumbing. The schedule needs to leave room for those visits. If a stage isn't ready or doesn't pass, the next trade waits.
- **In the estimate:** A good estimate should say which permits are needed, who is responsible for obtaining them, and how permit costs are handled. Fees are set by the county and change, so we don't quote them in articles. Pricing depends on scope, and a permit-required scope is part of that.

Build the permit stage into that timeline from the start, rather than treating it as a surprise.

## What does the county review, and how do you avoid plan delays?

The county reviews whether the proposed work is described clearly enough and meets current requirements. Most delays come from vague scopes, missing information or changes made after approval.

Ways to keep things moving:

1. **Define the scope before you apply.** Decide on layout, fixtures and major appliances first. Changing the plan mid-project can mean amending the permit.
2. **Get structural questions answered early.** If a wall might be coming out, get a licensed engineer's input before the application, not during demolition.
3. **Check for other approvals.** Some neighborhoods have HOA or community rules. Ask your association whether it requires its own approval.
4. **Don't close up walls before inspection.** Drywall over unapproved wiring or plumbing can mean tearing it back out.
5. **Keep the permit and approved plans on site.** The inspector expects to see work that matches what was approved.

## What should you ask your contractor about permits?

Ask direct questions before you sign. A contractor who handles permits routinely will answer them without hesitation.

- Is my project going to need a permit? Which ones (building, electrical, plumbing)?
- Who applies for and pays for the permits, and where does that show up in the estimate?
- Are the electrical and plumbing trades licensed, and will they be on site?
- How does the permit stage affect the start date and the overall schedule?
- When are the inspections, and what has to be ready for each?
- If the county asks for changes, how are those handled and priced?
- Is an engineer needed for any part of the scope, such as a wall change?
- Will I get copies of the permit and final approval at the end?

Also confirm the contractor's MHIC license. You can verify it with the state. ## FAQ

### Do I need a permit to replace kitchen cabinets and countertops in Baltimore County?

Usually not if you are replacing them in the same layout and nothing behind the walls changes. If the job also moves plumbing, adds circuits or alters walls, a permit is likely. Confirm with Baltimore County's permit office.

### Do I need a permit for a bathroom remodel in Baltimore County?

Often yes, when the work involves plumbing fixtures, new or moved electrical, or changes to walls or the layout. Cosmetic updates like paint, a new vanity top or flooring usually don't. Check with the county for your specific scope.

### Can I pull a remodeling permit myself as a homeowner?

Some jurisdictions allow it for work on your own home, but the rules vary and certain trades may need licensed professionals. Ask Baltimore County what applies to your project. If you hire a contractor, they normally handle it.

### What happens if I remodel without a permit?

It can lead to stop-work orders, requirements to open up finished work for inspection, and complications with insurance or when you sell. The county can tell you the specific consequences and options.

### Does the permit add time to my remodel?

Yes. There is a review stage before work starts and inspections during construction. How long varies by project and by the county's current workload, so ask for the current timeline when you plan.
