For most Baltimore County bathrooms, **porcelain tile** is the longest-lasting choice on floors and shower walls. It absorbs very little water, resists staining, and handles daily wear. Natural stone can last as long but needs sealing and upkeep. Whatever tile you pick, the waterproofing and setting work underneath it decides how long it lasts.

## Which tile lasts longest in a bathroom?

Porcelain, because it is dense, nearly waterproof, and tough enough for floors, shower walls, and tub surrounds. Ceramic works on walls, and natural stone, glass, and mosaics can all last when used in the right spot and installed correctly.

Here is how the common options compare in a bathroom:

- **Porcelain:** the workhorse. It comes in stone-look, wood-look, concrete-look, and classic subway formats, so you rarely have to give up style for durability. It is a good fit for floors, shower floors, and walls.
- **Standard ceramic:** softer and more porous than porcelain. It is fine on bathroom walls and backsplashes, but we are cautious about it on floors or in a shower that gets daily use, where chips show up sooner.
- **Natural stone (marble, travertine, slate, limestone):** beautiful, and it can last decades. It is also porous and reacts to acidic cleaners, so it needs sealing and the right cleaning habits. Marble in a heavily used shower is the choice we most often see homeowners regret.
- **Glass tile:** durable and easy to clean, but unforgiving to install. Any unevenness in the wall shows through, and the setting material has to be right for glass.
- **Large-format porcelain:** fewer grout lines means less to clean and less to fail, but it demands a flat substrate and an installer who knows how to handle big sheets.

If you want a tile that is forgiving for a busy household, porcelain is the safest answer. Choose stone or glass deliberately, knowing what upkeep comes with them.

## What should I look for on the floor versus the shower walls?

Floors need traction and wear resistance. Shower walls need to be easy to clean and to have as few vulnerable joints as possible.

### Floors

Slip resistance matters most where the floor gets wet. Check the manufacturer's spec sheet for the slip-resistance (DCOF) rating, and ask your contractor what they recommend for wet areas. A polished tile that looks great in a showroom can feel slick on a wet bathroom floor. A matte or lightly textured finish is usually a better daily-use choice, especially for a primary bath or a bathroom used by children or older family members.

If you want heated floors, porcelain conducts heat well. Talk about it at the design stage, because it affects the floor buildup and the electrical scope.

### Shower floors

The shower floor is where small tile earns its place. Small mosaics, or tile with more grout joints, give more grip on a sloped floor and follow the slope to the drain more easily than large tile does. The tradeoff is more grout to maintain. Some homeowners choose a large-format shower floor with a linear drain for a cleaner look, but that requires careful planning of the slope, so settle it before you buy tile.

### Walls

Walls give you the most freedom. Glass, glazed ceramic, and decorative porcelain all work. Think about how much cleaning you want to do. Narrow grout lines are easier to keep clean than wide ones, and a niche or bench built with tile that matches the walls looks more finished than a store-bought insert.

## What tile choices don't hold up?

The ones that fail most often are tiles used in the wrong place, not bad tiles. These are the combinations we steer clients away from:

- **Unsealed natural stone in a daily-use shower.** It stains and etches, and grout lines darken.
- **Polished, glossy tile on a wet floor.** It looks sharp and feels slippery.
- **Wall-rated tile on a floor.** Some tile is made only for walls. Check the manufacturer's rating before you buy anything for a floor.
- **Very thin or low-grade tile bought only on price.** Chips, warping, and inconsistent sizing make for an uneven install that is hard to grout well.
- **Heavily patterned layouts on a tight budget.** Herringbone, chevron, and intricate mosaics are lovely, but they add cuts and layout time. If the budget is tight, spend the money on a good installation of simpler tile.

Trends are the other risk. A bold color or pattern on every surface can date the room faster than a neutral tile with one accent area. ## Does the installation matter more than the tile?

Yes. A good porcelain tile installed over poor waterproofing will fail, and an average tile installed correctly will last. Most of the tile failures we are asked to look at are failures of what is under or between the tile, not the tile itself.

Because our team includes a former inspector, we know what gets flagged and what causes trouble later. These are the things we look at on every wet-area job:

1. **Waterproofing behind the tile.** Tile and grout are not waterproof on their own. The shower needs a proper waterproofing system behind and beneath the tile, with corners, seams, niches, and the shower floor all addressed.
2. **Slope to the drain.** A shower floor that does not pitch correctly leaves standing water, which means grout and tile problems over time.
3. **A flat, solid substrate.** Floors that flex or walls that are out of plane cause cracked tile and grout, and make large-format tile nearly impossible to set well.
4. **Movement joints and caulk.** Corners and changes of plane need flexible joints, not rigid grout. This is a common shortcut.
5. **The right setting materials.** Large tile, stone, and glass each have their own requirements for thinset and technique.

## How does tile choice affect cost and schedule?

Tile affects cost in two ways: the price of the tile itself, and the labor to install it. Pricing depends on scope, and we do not quote numbers without seeing the job.

Things that raise installation labor, regardless of the tile price:

- Large-format tile, which needs a flatter substrate and more careful handling
- Mosaics, patterns, and diagonal or herringbone layouts
- Tile run up to the ceiling, plus niches, benches, and curbs
- Natural stone, which needs sealing and often extra care in layout
- Special-order tile, which can affect the schedule if it arrives late

A lower-priced tile with simpler installation often leaves more budget for the parts you cannot see. ## Do I need a permit for a tile project?

It depends on the scope. Replacing tile alone is different from moving plumbing, changing the shower layout, or doing electrical work for heated floors. Baltimore County's permits and inspections office can tell you what applies to your project, and your contractor should be able to explain which permits your scope requires and handle them. Be wary of anyone who suggests skipping permits. You can also [verify a contractor's Maryland license](/blog/how-to-verify-a-maryland-contractor-license-before-hiring-anyone) before you hire.

If the plan involves changing a wall or the framing under a floor, that is a separate question that may need a structural engineer, not a tile decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is porcelain or ceramic better for a bathroom?

Porcelain is generally the better choice for floors and showers because it is denser and absorbs less water. Ceramic is fine for many bathroom walls.

### Can I use marble in a shower?

You can, but it needs sealing, gentle cleaners, and ongoing care. Many homeowners prefer porcelain that mimics marble for a low-maintenance shower.

### Should I choose large tile or small tile?

Large tile means fewer grout lines but needs a flatter surface. Small tile gives more grip on a shower floor but means more grout to clean. Many bathrooms use both.

### How long does bathroom tile last?

Well-installed porcelain over proper waterproofing can last for decades. How long depends on the installation, the use, and the care, and we do not promise a specific lifespan.

### Can you work from tile I already bought?

Often yes, but let us review it before installation. We check that it is rated for where you plan to use it and that you have enough to cover the cuts.
