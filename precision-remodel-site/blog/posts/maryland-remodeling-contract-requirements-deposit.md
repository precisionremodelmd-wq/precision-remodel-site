A Maryland remodeling contract should be in writing, signed by both parties, and spell out the contractor's name and MHIC license number, a detailed scope of work, the total price, a payment schedule, and start and finish dates. Maryland's Home Improvement Law also caps the down payment at one-third of the contract price. Confirm the current rule with MHIC.

## What should a Maryland remodeling contract include before you sign?

A Maryland home improvement contract should be written, signed by you and the contractor, and complete enough that a stranger could read it and know exactly what is being built, for how much, and by when. If something was promised verbally, it needs to be on the page.

The Maryland Home Improvement Commission publishes the rules contractors work under. Read its current guidance at [labor.maryland.gov/license/mhic](https://labor.maryland.gov/license/mhic/) rather than relying on a summary, including this one, because requirements can be updated. Use the table below as a working checklist and compare it to your draft.

| Contract item | What to look for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Contractor identity | Legal business name, address, phone, MHIC license number | Lets you verify the license and know who is responsible |
| Scope of work | Room-by-room description of tasks, not just "remodel kitchen" | Prevents disputes over what was included |
| Materials and products | Brands, models, finishes, sizes, or a clear allowance | Avoids substitutions and surprise upgrades |
| Total price | One clear contract price | Gives a baseline for any later change |
| Payment schedule | Amounts tied to work milestones, with the deposit stated | Keeps payments in step with progress |
| Dates | Approximate start and completion dates | Sets expectations and gives you a reference if work stalls |
| Change-order process | Changes priced and signed in writing before the work | Stops verbal add-ons from becoming billing fights |
| Permits | Who applies for them and who pays | Avoids unpermitted work |
| Warranty | What is covered, for how long, and who to call | Defines your recourse after the job |
| Required notices | Any notices MHIC requires, such as Guaranty Fund information | Shows the contractor is following the state's rules |

If your draft is missing several of these, ask for a revised version before you sign. A contractor who resists putting details in writing is telling you something.

## Scope of work, allowances and change orders: where disputes start

Most remodeling disputes come from a vague scope, an unclear allowance, or a change that was agreed verbally. Fixing those three things in writing prevents most of them.

**Scope of work.** "Remodel master bath" is not a scope. A useful scope lists demolition, any framing or plumbing relocation, electrical work, waterproofing, tile areas, fixtures, paint, and cleanup. It also says what is *not* included, such as new flooring in the adjoining hall or repairs if hidden damage is found.

**Allowances.** An allowance is a placeholder budget for items you have not chosen yet, such as tile, plumbing fixtures, or cabinet hardware. Ask three questions: what exactly does the allowance cover (material only, or material and installation)? What happens if you pick something cheaper? What happens if you pick something more expensive?

Unrealistically low allowances are a common way to make a bid look cheaper than it is. On high-end kitchens and bathrooms, where selections drive the budget, compare allowances line by line across bids.

**Change orders.** Changes happen on almost every remodel, especially once walls are open. The contract should say that any change in scope, materials, or price is written up, priced, and signed by both parties before the work proceeds. If a change might affect structure, such as moving or removing a wall, it may also need an engineer's input. Our article on [removing a load-bearing wall](https://precisionremodelingmd.com/blog/removing-a-load-bearing-wall-cost-permits-process) explains how that process affects cost and permits.

Also ask how hidden conditions are handled. Rot behind a tub, outdated wiring, or an unlevel subfloor can surface after demolition. A good contract describes how those findings will be documented, priced, and approved rather than leaving it to a conversation on the porch.

## How much deposit should a remodeling contractor ask for in Maryland?

Maryland limits the down payment on a home improvement contract to one-third of the contract price. That is the state's rule as we understand it, so confirm the current wording with MHIC before you sign, and treat one-third as a ceiling rather than a target.

A few practical points on deposits and payments:

- **The cap applies to the deposit, not the whole schedule.** After the down payment, payments should follow the work actually completed.
- **Special-order items can complicate things.** Custom cabinets, windows, or stone slabs may be ordered before work starts. If a contractor asks for extra money upfront for materials, ask them to point to the specific rule that allows it, and check with MHIC.
- **A smaller deposit is fine.** The cap is not a minimum. Plenty of reasonable contractors ask for less.
- **Pay by a traceable method** such as check or card, and get a written receipt for every payment.
- **Never pay in full upfront**, and never let the payments run ahead of the work.

A milestone-based schedule usually looks like the outline below. The exact stages and amounts depend on your scope and the contractor, so this is a structure to look for, not a price list.

| Stage | Typical trigger | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit | Signing the contract | Within the state's cap; receipt provided |
| Early progress payment | Demolition and rough-in complete | Work visible and matches scope |
| Mid-job payment | Inspections passed, major installation done | Permit inspections signed off where required |
| Final payment | Punch list complete, final inspection passed | Don't release it until the list is done |

Pricing varies with scope, selections, and the condition of the existing house, so we do not quote a standard deposit amount. We quote after reviewing the project. ## How do you confirm a contractor's MHIC license before paying?

Look up the contractor's license number on the MHIC website before you sign or pay. Confirm that the name on the contract matches the name on the license and that the license is active.

Here is a simple routine:

1. Find the MHIC license number on the contract, estimate, and truck or website.
2. Search it through the MHIC licensing pages at [labor.maryland.gov/license/mhic](https://labor.maryland.gov/license/mhic/).
3. Check that the business name matches, the license is current, and there are no disciplinary issues you are uncomfortable with.
4. Ask whether the person you are dealing with works for the licensed company.

MHIC also explains how its Guaranty Fund works for homeowners who suffer a loss from a licensed contractor's work. Eligibility and claim limits are set by the state, so read the details there rather than assuming coverage. Our own license is MHIC #151439, and you are welcome to look us up.

A license is a minimum. It does not tell you how well a contractor communicates, schedules, or finishes, so also ask for references and recent local projects.

## Warranty, permits and who pulls them

Your contract should say who applies for permits, who pays for them, and what warranty applies after the work is done. These are the two places where vague contracts cost homeowners the most later.

**Permits.** Many kitchen and bathroom projects need permits, especially when they involve electrical, plumbing, structural changes, or layout moves. Requirements differ by county and by scope. In Baltimore County, check with the county's Permits, Approvals and Inspections office; other counties and municipalities have their own offices and rules. Ask your contractor which permits your project needs and have the answer in writing.

In general, you want the contractor to handle permits for the work they perform and to schedule the inspections. Be cautious if a contractor suggests you pull the permit yourself to save time or cost, or suggests skipping it. Unpermitted work can create problems when you sell, refinance, or file an insurance claim, and a future home inspector or buyer's inspector may flag it. Our inspector background is one reason we plan inspections into the schedule from day one.

**Warranty.** Ask for the warranty in writing. A useful warranty states:

- What workmanship is covered and for how long
- What is handled by manufacturers instead (appliances, fixtures, tile, cabinets)
- How to request service, and who to call
- What voids coverage, such as misuse or unrelated damage

The length and terms of a remodeling warranty are set by the contract, so compare them across bids. If a contractor says "we stand behind our work" but will not write it down, treat that as no warranty.

## What are the red flags in a remodeling contract or estimate?

The biggest red flags are pressure to sign fast, a large payment upfront, a missing license number, and a vague scope. Any one of these is reason to slow down.

- **No MHIC license number**, or a number that does not match the business name.
- **A deposit above what Maryland allows**, or a request for most of the money before work begins.
- **Cash-only payment**, or a request to make checks out to an individual.
- **A one-line scope** such as "bathroom remodel, one price."
- **No start or completion dates**, or dates that are "flexible" with no explanation.
- **"Skip the permit"** or a suggestion that you pull it yourself without explanation.
- **Pressure tactics**, like a price that is only good today.
- **A bid far below the others.** Check what is missing from the scope or allowances before assuming it's a bargain.
- **Verbal promises** about materials, warranty, or extras that are not in the document.

If you signed a contract at your home after a door-to-door pitch or unsolicited solicitation, separate cancellation rights may apply. Ask MHIC or the Maryland Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division about your situation. If you are unsure whether a contract is fair, an attorney can review it before you sign.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a remodeling contract have to be in writing in Maryland?

Yes. Maryland's Home Improvement Law requires home improvement contracts to be in writing, and you should insist on a signed copy for your records. Check the MHIC website for the current list of required contents.

### How much deposit can a remodeling contractor ask for in Maryland?

The state caps the down payment at one-third of the contract price. That is a maximum, not a standard, and many contractors ask for less. Confirm the current rule with MHIC and ask about any special-order material arrangements in writing.

### How do I check if a Maryland contractor is licensed?

Search the contractor's MHIC license number on the Maryland Home Improvement Commission website. Make sure the license is active and the name matches the business on your contract.

### Who is responsible for pulling permits on a remodel?

It should be stated in your contract. Generally the contractor performing the work pulls the permits and schedules the inspections. Permit requirements vary by county and scope, so ask the local permit office, such as Baltimore County's Permits, Approvals and Inspections, what your project needs.
