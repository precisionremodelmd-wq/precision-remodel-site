Opening Up a Kitchen to the Dining Room in Baltimore: Planning, Engineer Questions and Order of Work

November 11, 2026  ·  10 min read

Yes, in most Baltimore-area homes the wall between a kitchen and dining room can come out, but only after someone qualified confirms what the wall carries. A structural engineer makes that call, the cost depends on scope (beam, utilities, finishes), and a permit is typically needed before demolition.

Can you remove the wall between a kitchen and dining room?

Usually yes, but the wall itself does not decide the answer. What sits above it, what runs inside it, and what the floor and foundation below it can support do.

We see three common situations in Baltimore rowhomes and Baltimore County single-family homes around Towson, Parkville and Catonsville:

  • A partition wall that carries nothing. These are the simplest to remove, though they still often hide wiring, ductwork or plumbing.
  • A wall that carries floor or roof framing. It can often be replaced with a beam, but the beam size, the posts, and the load path to the foundation need an engineer's design.
  • A wall that is partly structural. This happens in older homes where past renovations changed the framing. These are the ones that surprise people.

If you want to learn how to read the clues yourself, our guides on how to tell if a wall is load-bearing and what makes a wall load-bearing cover that. This article is about planning the project once you have decided you want the space open.

Why does an engineer decide whether a wall can come out?

The engineer decides because the answer is a calculation, not a guess from the outside. A wall that looks ordinary can be carrying a floor, a roof, or a point load from above.

An engineer typically looks at:

  • What the wall supports above (a second floor, a roofline, a bearing point from another wall)
  • The span you want to open and the size and type of beam that spans it
  • Where the posts at each end will land and what carries that load down to the foundation
  • Whether the beam can sit in the ceiling (flush) or has to hang below it (dropped)

A flush beam looks cleaner but usually costs more, because it involves more framing work. A dropped beam is simpler but changes how the room looks. That is a design decision you should make with your contractor before the engineer finalizes the plan.

We are not engineers, and we do not give structural opinions. We bring the engineer in early, and we build to the engineer's drawings. Your permit office may also ask for those drawings. For more on this process, see removing a load-bearing wall: cost, permits and process.

How much does it cost to open up a kitchen wall?

There is no honest flat number, because the cost depends on scope. Two walls the same width can differ widely in price depending on what is inside them and what has to be rebuilt around the opening.

Here is what moves the price, and who can tell you more:

  • Structural or not: A beam, posts and footings add engineering and framing (ask: structural engineer)
  • Beam type and placement: Flush beams take more work than dropped beams (ask: engineer and contractor)
  • Utilities in the wall: Rerouting plumbing, electrical or ductwork adds trades (ask: contractor)
  • Width of the opening: A wider span means a larger beam and more finish work (ask: engineer)
  • Floor, ceiling and wall patching: Flooring often has to be matched across the opening (ask: contractor)
  • Cabinets, counters, and layout changes: An open plan often triggers a new kitchen layout (ask: designer or contractor)
  • Permits and inspections: Requirements vary by jurisdiction (ask: your permit office)

The biggest mistake we see is homeowners budgeting for the wall alone. Opening the wall usually changes the flooring, the ceiling, the lighting, and the kitchen layout around it. If you are budgeting for an open-concept kitchen remodel in Baltimore or Towson, plan for the whole room, not just the demolition.

When you collect bids, ask each contractor to list what is included: engineering, permit, beam, utility rerouting, patching, flooring, and paint. That is how you compare real numbers.

What utilities are in the wall, and why do they change the scope?

Utilities in the wall are the most common reason a simple-looking opening becomes a bigger project. Anything running through the wall has to be moved, and where it goes next affects other rooms.

Common things we find inside kitchen and dining room walls:

  • Electrical wiring, including circuits that feed other rooms, not just the kitchen
  • Plumbing, such as supply lines, drains or a vent stack
  • Ductwork for heating and cooling, or a return
  • Gas lines, which have to be handled by a licensed professional

A vent or drain stack can be the hardest to move, because it has to keep running in a way that works from the basement to the roof. Ductwork can sometimes be rerouted into a soffit or dropped ceiling, which affects your design.

You often cannot tell what is in a wall until it is opened, so a good contractor builds in an allowance and tells you where the unknowns are.

An inspector's background helps here: at Precision Remodel, we know the kinds of things that get found behind old walls, and we plan for them rather than being surprised by them.

What permits and inspections apply in Baltimore County?

Structural changes generally need a permit, and the requirements depend on where you live. Baltimore City and Baltimore County are separate jurisdictions with separate offices, so confirm which one your address falls under before you plan the timeline.

What varies, and who to ask:

  • Whether a permit is required and what drawings it needs. Ask your county or city permit office.
  • Whether engineered drawings must be submitted. The permit office will tell you; your engineer will prepare them.
  • Electrical, plumbing or mechanical permits for rerouting work. These often run separately from the main permit.
  • Historic or neighborhood rules, if your home is in a historic district or has an HOA.

We do not quote permit fees, because they change and depend on the project.

At the framing stage, an inspector typically wants to see the structural work before it is covered. That means the beam, the posts, the connections and the support below, checked against the approved plan.

Having worked as an inspector, our team builds with that visit in mind: the framing is accessible, labeled to match the drawings, and ready to be looked at. Covering work before it is inspected is a good way to have to open it up again.

What layout decisions should you make before asking for bids?

Decide how open you want the space, because that drives the structural and utility scope. Contractors can only compare bids fairly if you give them the same starting point.

Before you request bids, think through:

  1. Fully open or partially open? A wide opening, a half wall, or a cased opening each have different structural needs.
  2. Where will the kitchen work zones go? Removing a wall often frees up cabinet runs, an island, or a peninsula, and may expose a window or two.
  3. Where do the appliances and sink go? Moving the sink or range means moving plumbing, gas, or venting.
  4. What happens to the floor? If the two rooms have different flooring, decide whether to replace one or both.
  5. How will the range hood vent? In an open plan, ventilation matters more, and your layout affects the route.
  6. What about lighting and outlets? A wall removal takes away places where outlets and switches used to be.

What is the order of work from engineer to demolition?

The sequence matters: engineer first, permit second, demolition last. Starting demolition before the structure is confirmed is the most expensive way to find out you needed a plan.

  1. Site visit and scope. We walk the house, talk through the layout you want, and note what we can see about the wall and what is above and below it.
  2. Engineer. A structural engineer evaluates the wall and designs the beam, posts and supports.
  3. Design decisions. Flush or dropped beam, layout, finishes and utility routes are settled.
  4. Permit. The drawings and application go to the permit office. Timing varies by jurisdiction.
  5. Temporary support and demolition. Temporary walls carry the load while the wall comes out and the beam goes in.
  6. Framing and rough-in. The beam and posts are installed, and utilities are rerouted.
  7. Framing inspection. The inspector checks the structural work before it is closed up.
  8. Finish work. Drywall, ceiling, flooring, cabinets, electrical trim and paint.

Your order may shift depending on the house, but the engineer-then-permit-then-demolition sequence should not. If you also have concerns about movement or settlement in the house, our structural repairs page explains how we handle those separately.

FAQ

How much does it cost to open up a kitchen wall in a Baltimore rowhome?

It depends on scope. The main drivers are whether the wall is structural, the size of the beam and its placement, what has to be rerouted inside the wall, and how much flooring, ceiling and layout work follows. Ask contractors for a written breakdown that lists engineering, permit, beam, utilities and finishes.

Do I need an engineer to remove a kitchen wall?

If there is any chance the wall carries load, yes. A structural engineer determines what the wall supports and designs the replacement beam and posts. If the wall is confirmed non-structural, you may not need one, but have that confirmed rather than assumed.

Do I need a permit to open a wall between the kitchen and dining room?

Usually, yes, especially for structural changes or rerouting of utilities. Requirements differ between Baltimore City and Baltimore County, so confirm with your local permit office before work starts.

Can a rowhome kitchen wall be removed?

Often it can, but rowhomes frequently have joists bearing on the party walls and sometimes on interior walls too, so each case needs an engineer's review. Do not assume based on the neighbor's house; framing varies even on the same block.

Does opening the wall mean I have to redo my kitchen?

Not always, but it often leads to it. Cabinets, flooring, lighting and ventilation are commonly affected, so it is worth planning the wall and the kitchen together.
