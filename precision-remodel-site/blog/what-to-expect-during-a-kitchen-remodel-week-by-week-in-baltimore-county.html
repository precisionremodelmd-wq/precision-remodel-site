What to expect during a kitchen remodel — week by week in Baltimore County

August 4, 2026  ·  7 min read

A kitchen remodel in Baltimore County usually moves through the same sequence: planning and ordering before any work starts, then demolition, rough-in work, inspections, drywall, cabinets, countertops, finish work and a punch list. Calendar time depends on scope, permits and material lead times, so treat the weeks below as an order of events, not a promise.

For a total-time estimate based on scope, see how long a kitchen remodel takes in Baltimore County. This article walks through what is actually happening in your house, and in your life, at each stage.

What happens before week one?

Most of the schedule is decided before demolition. Design, selections, permits and ordering come first, and this stage often takes longer than the construction itself.

Here is what should be settled before anyone swings a hammer:

  • Layout and scope. Are you keeping the footprint, moving the sink or range, or opening a wall? Each choice changes the plumbing, electrical and permit work.
  • Selections. Cabinets, counters, appliances, tile, plumbing fixtures and lighting need to be chosen early. Custom or special-order items can have long lead times. If you are weighing options, see custom cabinetry vs stock cabinets and quartz vs granite.
  • Permits. Baltimore County's Department of Permits, Approvals and Inspections (PAI) handles permit applications. Whether your project needs permits, and which ones, depends on the scope. Ask PAI or your contractor to confirm for your address.
  • A written scope and estimate. Use our line-by-line estimate checklist to make sure bids are comparable.

If a wall might be coming out, this is also when you need an answer on whether it carries load. That is a question for a structural engineer, not a guess. See removing a load-bearing wall for how that process works.

What happens in week one: demolition and setup?

Week one is protection, disconnecting and demolition. It is the loudest and messiest stretch, but also the quickest to show progress.

A typical first few days:

  1. Floors, doorways and nearby rooms are covered, and dust barriers go up.
  2. Water, gas and electrical lines to the kitchen are shut off and disconnected by the appropriate trade.
  3. Cabinets, counters, appliances and any flooring or walls in the scope are removed.
  4. Debris goes out, and the room is swept down to studs and subfloor.

This is when surprises show up. In the older homes around Essex, Rosedale and Parkville, opened walls and floors sometimes reveal old wiring, past patch jobs, water damage or framing that doesn't match the drawings. In our experience, a good contractor tells you quickly, shows you photos and explains what it means for scope and cost, rather than quietly absorbing it or springing it on you at the end. Any change should go in writing before the work proceeds.

Set up your temporary kitchen before demolition day. A microwave, a coffee maker, a small fridge and paper goods in another room make the following weeks much easier. Many homeowners in Perry Hall and White Marsh use a basement or dining room.

What are the rough-in and inspection weeks?

Once the room is open, the plumber, electrician and any HVAC or framing trades do their rough-in work, and inspections happen before walls are closed. This stage is invisible to most people but matters most.

Rough-in typically includes:

  • New or relocated plumbing supply and drain lines.
  • New circuits, outlets, lighting boxes and wiring for appliances.
  • Venting for a range hood, usually to the exterior.
  • Any framing changes, such as a new header or reworked soffit.

Where permits apply, the county inspector looks at this work before drywall covers it. Inspection scheduling is outside the contractor's full control, so this is one place a schedule can slip. Our team includes a licensed home inspector, so we know what inspectors flag: unprotected wires, improper box fill, missing or incorrect venting, drain slope problems and unsupported lines. We plan the rough-in to pass the first time instead of discovering problems after the walls are closed. Anyone telling you to skip the permit to save time is giving you bad advice, and unpermitted work can complicate a future sale.

What happens when walls, floors and cabinets go in?

After rough-in passes, the kitchen starts to look like a kitchen again: insulation if needed, drywall, paint priming, flooring and then cabinet installation.

The order matters:

  1. Drywall: hanging, taping and finishing takes several visits because the compound must dry between coats.
  2. Prime and ceiling or wall paint: best done before cabinets so installers don't damage finished boxes.
  3. Flooring: installed before or after cabinets depending on the material and design. Your contractor should tell you which approach they use and why.
  4. Cabinets: set, leveled, shimmed and fastened. Cabinets need to be level and plumb because every countertop, backsplash and appliance sits on that work.

Cabinet arrival is a common friction point. If a single box is damaged or wrong, it can hold up the next steps. That is why we recommend checking deliveries on arrival and storing them before demolition, not after.

Why is there a gap between cabinets and countertops?

Most stone and quartz countertops can't be fabricated until the installed cabinets are measured. A templating visit happens after cabinets are set, then the fabricator cuts the slabs to match.

That gap is often several days to more than a week, depending on the fabricator's schedule and the material, so ask for their current turnaround. During this gap, other work can continue: finish electrical, trim carpentry, paint touch-ups and appliance staging. But the sink and cooktop can't be connected until the counters go in, so your temporary kitchen stays in use.

After counters, the sequence usually goes:

  • Sink, faucet and dishwasher connections.
  • Backsplash tile and grout.
  • Light fixtures, switches and outlets with cover plates.
  • Range hood, appliances, hardware and trim.

What does the final stretch look like?

The last phase is a punch list, final inspection where required, cleaning and a walkthrough with you. It is detail work, but it determines how the kitchen feels for years.

Walk the room with your project lead and look for:

  • Doors and drawers that rub or don't close evenly.
  • Caulk lines, paint touch-ups and grout consistency.
  • Appliances operating correctly, including the hood and dishwasher.
  • Outlets, switches and under-cabinet lighting all working.

Write the list down, agree on who fixes what and by when, and don't release final payment until the list is complete. If a final inspection is required for your permit, confirm it is scheduled and passed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I stay in my house during a kitchen remodel?

Most homeowners do. Expect dust, noise, and days without water or power in the kitchen area. Plan a temporary kitchen and ask your contractor about daily cleanup and dust control.

What most often delays a kitchen remodel?

Backed-up deliveries, special-order items, inspection scheduling, countertop fabrication time and mid-project changes. Hidden conditions in older walls and floors can also add work.

Does the contractor handle permits?

A reputable contractor usually pulls the permits and schedules the inspections. Confirm this in your contract and verify the license first. Our guide to verifying a Maryland contractor license shows how.

Should I make changes once work has started?

Small changes are manageable, but moving plumbing, electrical or cabinets after rough-in is costly and can affect the schedule. Finalize the layout and selections before demolition and put any change in writing.
