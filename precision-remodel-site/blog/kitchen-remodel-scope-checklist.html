Kitchen Remodel Scope Checklist: What to Decide Before You Ask for Bids

October 29, 2026  ·  12 min read

A kitchen remodel scope defines what is changing, what is staying, and which products you have chosen. It covers layout, structural changes, plumbing, electrical, ventilation, cabinets, counters, appliances, lighting, permits, and timing. The more you settle before asking for bids, the more the quotes will compare like for like.

What goes into a kitchen remodel scope of work?

A kitchen remodel scope of work is a written list of every task, material, and responsibility in the project, including who handles permits and what is excluded. It is the document bids are priced from, so gaps in it become surprises in the price.

Most disputes between homeowners and contractors trace back to the scope. One bid includes new circuits and the next quietly assumes the old wiring stays. One includes a vent hood duct to the exterior and another prices only the hood. Neither contractor is necessarily wrong. They were just pricing different kitchens.

At Precision Remodel, we are MHIC #151439 licensed, and our background in inspection shapes how we write scopes: we know what inspectors flag, so we put those items on paper before work starts rather than discovering them at the rough-in inspection.

The sections below follow the order we use when planning a kitchen with a homeowner. If you want to see how we approach full kitchen projects, visit our kitchens page.

Are you changing the layout, and does anything structural come with it?

Decide first whether the layout stays or changes, because it drives nearly every other cost. Keeping the sink, range, and refrigerator where they are is a very different project from moving them or opening a wall.

Write down answers to these:

  • Are appliances and the sink staying in their current locations?
  • Are you adding an island or peninsula? If so, will it need power, a sink, or a cooktop?
  • Are you removing or opening a wall to the dining or living room?
  • Are you changing window or door openings?
  • Are you moving the pantry, or adding a pantry closet?

A wall that looks like a simple partition may be carrying roof or floor loads. Do not assume either way. A contractor can tell you what they see and what is typical, but a load-bearing determination and the size of any replacement beam is an engineering question. If a wall comes out, plan on a licensed structural engineer being involved and on that cost being its own line in the scope. Our guides on how to tell if a wall is load-bearing and removing a load-bearing wall: cost, permits and process explain what to expect.

The scope should also state who arranges the engineer, who pays for the drawings, and whether the contractor's price includes the beam, temporary shoring, and finishing the ceiling afterward.

What plumbing, electrical and ventilation work should be in the scope?

List every fixture, circuit, and duct you expect, and say whether each is new, relocated, or reused. These three trades hide the most price differences between bids.

Plumbing

  • Sink location and type (single, double, farmhouse, undermount), and whether the base cabinet is being modified for it
  • Dishwasher, disposal, and any filtered or pot-filler water lines
  • Refrigerator water line for ice and water
  • Replacement of old shutoff valves and supply lines while walls are open
  • Gas line work if you are adding or moving a range or cooktop

Electrical

  • Whether the existing panel has room for the new circuits, or needs evaluation by an electrician
  • Dedicated circuits for the range or cooktop, microwave, dishwasher, disposal, and refrigerator
  • Countertop receptacles and island outlets
  • Whether older ungrounded or outdated wiring will be replaced or left
  • Under-cabinet and recessed lighting circuits and switching

Electrical is one of the places where age matters. In many older Baltimore County houses, kitchens were wired for fewer appliances than a modern kitchen uses. Ask any contractor to tell you in writing what they found, what they are assuming, and what happens to the price if the wiring behind the walls turns out different.

Ventilation

  • Hood type: ducted to the exterior or recirculating
  • Duct route and exterior termination point, especially when the range moves to an interior wall or an island
  • Hood size and the fan's airflow rating for the cooktop you chose

A ducted hood is one of the most common items we see left out of scopes. Running a duct through a ceiling or up through a second floor takes carpentry and sometimes patching in rooms outside the kitchen. Decide early, and have it written down.

What cabinet, counter, appliance and lighting decisions should be made before bidding?

Choose the products, or at least the category and tier, before bids come in. If you have not, the scope should use clearly labeled allowances so every contractor prices the same budget for the same items.

Cabinets

  • Stock, semi-custom, or custom, and the brand or cabinet shop if you have chosen one
  • Door style, finish, and hardware, and who supplies the hardware
  • Cabinet height, crown molding, and whether cabinets go to the ceiling
  • Pull-outs, trash drawers, spice storage, and other interior features, which are easy to forget and add up

Counters and backsplash

  • Material (quartz, granite, soapstone, butcher block, and so on), edge profile, and thickness
  • Sink cutout and cooktop cutout, and who handles them
  • Backsplash material, height, and where it stops
  • Whether the fabricator measures after cabinets are set, which is the normal practice

Appliances

  • Exact models, or at least dimensions, before cabinets are ordered; a cabinet opening built around the wrong appliance causes costly rework
  • Who buys the appliances and who is responsible for delivery damage or returns
  • Panel-ready refrigerators or dishwashers, which need cabinet-matched panels

Lighting

  • Recessed, pendants, under-cabinet, and toe-kick lighting, and where each switch goes
  • Who supplies the fixtures
  • Dimmers and control types

If you do not know the exact product yet, an allowance is acceptable, but it must be labeled. Pricing depends on scope and on the choices you make. For example, a bid that says tile backsplash with no material allowance can't be compared with one that carries a specific allowance per square foot. Ask each contractor to state their allowances line by line.

What permits and inspections does a kitchen remodel need?

Permit requirements vary by the type of work and by jurisdiction, so confirm with your local permitting office rather than relying on a general rule. For homes in Baltimore County, that is the county's permits and inspections office; other counties and the City of Baltimore have their own.

Work that often triggers permits and inspections includes moving walls, new or relocated electrical circuits, plumbing changes, gas line work, and mechanical items such as ducted ventilation. Cosmetic replacement, such as painting or swapping like-for-like finishes, is often treated differently. Do not guess. Ask the permit office or your contractor which items in your scope require permits, and have it written in the scope.

Here is what the scope should state about permits:

QuestionWhy it matters
Who pulls the permits?The contractor of record normally handles it; if they ask you to pull them as owner, understand what responsibility you are taking on
Are permit fees included or passed through?Fees are set by the jurisdiction and vary, so ask for them listed separately
Which inspections are expected?Rough-in and final inspections are typical, but your jurisdiction decides
Who is present for inspections?Someone needs to give access; know who is scheduling
What if an inspection fails?The contractor should be responsible for corrections within their scope

Unpermitted work can cause trouble later when you sell or when an insurer asks questions. As a former inspector's perspective: a buyer's inspector looks for evidence of renovations, and a kitchen with no permit history on new wiring or a relocated gas line draws attention. That is a reason to keep permits in the scope even when it feels like paperwork.

How long will it take, and in what order does the work happen?

A kitchen remodel follows a fairly consistent sequence, and the schedule depends mostly on lead times for cabinets, countertops, and appliances, plus inspections. Your scope should include a sequence and a realistic start date.

A typical order is:

  1. Planning, product selection, and permits
  2. Ordering cabinets and long-lead items
  3. Demolition and protection of the rest of the house
  4. Structural work, if any, then framing changes
  5. Rough-in plumbing, electrical, and ventilation, followed by rough inspections
  6. Insulation, drywall, and paint prep
  7. Flooring, if it goes under cabinets
  8. Cabinet installation
  9. Countertop templating and installation
  10. Backsplash, plumbing and electrical trim-out, lighting, appliances
  11. Final inspection, punch list, and cleaning

Countertops are normally measured after cabinets are installed, which creates a gap in the schedule. Ask each contractor how they handle that gap, and whether you will have a working sink in the meantime. Also decide how you will live during the project: whether you need a temporary kitchen setup, which room it will be in, and whether pets or children affect work hours.

FAQ

What should I decide first when planning a kitchen remodel?

Decide whether the layout stays or changes. Moving the sink, range, or refrigerator, or opening a wall, drives plumbing, electrical, structural, and permit work, and so affects almost every other line in the scope and the price.

Do I need to pick every finish before I ask contractors for bids?

Not every finish, but you should pick major ones or set clearly labeled allowances. Without them, contractors assume different budgets for cabinets, counters, tile, and fixtures, and the bids cannot be compared fairly.

Do I need an engineer for my kitchen remodel?

Only if the work affects the structure, such as removing or opening a wall that may be load-bearing, or changing a beam or opening. A licensed structural engineer makes that determination, not a contractor or a general article, so include it in the scope.

Do kitchen remodels need permits in Baltimore County?

Often, when the work involves electrical, plumbing, gas, structural, or mechanical changes, but requirements vary by project and jurisdiction. Confirm with the Baltimore County permits office or your local one, and put who pulls the permits in writing.

How many contractors should I get bids from?

Two to three well-matched bids are usually enough when every contractor receives the same written scope. More bids built from vague scopes tend to create confusion instead of clarity.
