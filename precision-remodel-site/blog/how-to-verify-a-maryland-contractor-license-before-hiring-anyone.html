How to verify a Maryland contractor license before hiring anyone

July 21, 2026  ·  7 min read

Look up the contractor's name or license number in the Maryland Home Improvement Commission (MHIC) online license search, run by the Maryland Department of Labor. Confirm the license is active, the name matches your contract, and the number is printed on the contract. Then verify insurance separately, because a license doesn't prove coverage.

Who needs an MHIC license in Maryland?

In general, anyone who is paid to remodel, repair, or improve an existing home in Maryland needs a Home Improvement Commission license. That covers kitchen and bathroom remodels, additions, finished basements, and similar work. Exemptions and classifications exist, so if a contractor says they don't need one, ask them to put that in writing and then confirm with the Commission.

One point that surprises homeowners: the license belongs to a person or a business entity, not to a truck or a logo. The name you check has to be the name you are actually signing with.

How do I look up an MHIC license, step by step?

The check takes about five minutes if you have the right details in hand. Here is the order we'd follow as a homeowner.

  1. Get the details in writing. Ask for the contractor's legal business name, the owner's name, and the MHIC license number. A real contractor gives these up without hesitation.
  2. Open the state's license search. Search for "Maryland MHIC license search" and use the Maryland Department of Labor's own page, not a third-party directory. Search by license number first, then by name as a second check.
  3. Match the record to your paperwork. The name on the record should match the name on the estimate and the contract. A different company name or a different owner is a reason to stop and ask questions.
  4. Check the status. Look for an active license and a current expiration date. An expired, suspended, or revoked license means no signing and no deposit.
  5. Check the classification. Confirm the license is for home improvement work like yours.
  6. Ask about complaint history. The Commission handles complaints and can tell you what is public and how to request it. Its website explains the process.

If anything on the record doesn't line up, call the Commission. They are the authority on what a record means, not the contractor and not us.

Does a valid license mean the contractor is insured?

No. A license and insurance are separate things, and you need to check both. Some older advice, including the previous version of this post, lumps them together. Don't assume one covers the other.

Ask for a certificate of insurance that lists general liability coverage, and ask whether the contractor carries workers' compensation for employees. Then call the insurance agent or carrier listed on the certificate to confirm the policy is active. A certificate can be out of date, so a quick call is worth it. Ask to be named as the certificate holder if you want notice of cancellation.

What coverage amounts are appropriate varies with the project and your own homeowner's policy. If you're unsure, your insurance agent is the right person to ask.

What about electricians, plumbers, and other subcontractors?

Electrical and plumbing work generally requires separate trade licenses from the state, in addition to the home improvement license held by the general contractor. In a kitchen or bathroom remodel, both trades almost always come into play.

Ask your contractor three questions:

  • Who is doing the electrical and plumbing, employees or subcontractors?
  • What are their license numbers?
  • Who pulls the permits for that work?

The relevant state licensing boards have their own lookups. Verify those the same way you would the MHIC record. If you want to see how the trades fit into the sequence of a job, our week-by-week kitchen remodel guide shows where each one comes in.

What should the contract say about the license?

A Maryland home improvement contract should be in writing and should include the contractor's MHIC license number, along with a clear scope, price, and payment schedule. If your contract has no license number, that is a red flag by itself, even if the contractor tells you they're licensed.

Before signing, check these points:

  • The legal name and license number match the state record.
  • The scope of work is specific enough to compare against other bids. Our line-by-line estimate checklist walks through that.
  • The deposit and payment schedule are tied to progress, not a large payment up front. Maryland law limits how much a contractor can ask for as a deposit. Check the current limit with the Commission instead of relying on a number from a blog post.
  • Permits are listed, along with who is responsible for them.

The Commission also operates a guaranty fund that may reimburse homeowners for certain losses caused by a licensed contractor's poor work. Eligibility, limits, and the process are set by the state, so read the Commission's information directly. That protection is a good reason to hire a contractor with a real, active license.

What red flags should I watch for?

Most problems show up before the contract is signed. Watch for these:

  • A license number that is missing from the estimate or contract, or one that is "pending"
  • A name on the license that is different from the person you're dealing with
  • "You don't need a permit for this" for work that obviously involves plumbing, electrical, or structure
  • Pressure to pay a big deposit or pay in cash
  • No certificate of insurance, or reluctance to let you verify it
  • A bid far below the others with no explanation of what's left out

Permits matter here. Baltimore County's permits office, Permits, Approvals and Inspections, is the place to confirm what your project requires. Unpermitted work can cause trouble at resale and at inspection. As a team with a home-inspection background, we know what inspectors flag, and missing permits and unlicensed trade work are on that list. A contractor who is relaxed about permits is telling you something about how the rest of the job will go. If your project touches a wall, see removing a load-bearing wall for why an engineer gets involved. That is a call for a licensed engineer, not for us or any contractor to make by eye.

Frequently asked questions

Is the MHIC license search free?

The state's online license lookup is publicly available. Use the Maryland Department of Labor's official page, and call the Commission if you need records the online search doesn't show.

Can a contractor use someone else's license number?

It happens, which is why you match the name on the record to the name on your contract and to the person who shows up. If the names differ, ask for an explanation and confirm it with the Commission.

Should I verify a license even if a neighbor recommends the contractor?

Yes. A referral tells you about past work, not current license status or insurance. Licenses lapse, and the check is quick.

Is a handyman covered by the same rules?

It depends on the work. Many projects that people call handyman jobs fall under home improvement licensing, especially anything bigger than minor repairs. Ask the Commission whether your project needs a licensed contractor.
